00 // Overview
Scientific Examination
of Paintings

Paintings are stratified physical objects -- ground, priming, underdrawing, paint layers, varnish -- and each layer encodes information about materials, technique, and time. The forensic examination of paintings draws on physics, analytical chemistry, dendrochronology, and, increasingly, machine learning to extract that information without physical sampling.

This reference covers methods from X-ray radiography and infrared reflectography through hyperspectral imaging, MA-XRF, Raman spectroscopy, neutron autoradiography, dendrochronology, and GC/MS medium analysis, concluding with the emerging deep learning pipeline that is beginning to automate pigment mapping and attribution.

All technical claims in this document are drawn from peer-reviewed primary literature and have been independently verified. Confidence ratings follow a three-vote adversarial protocol. Citations link to primary sources; a full bibliography appears at the end.

01 // Spectral Imaging
Multispectral and Hyperspectral Imaging

Physics and Setup

Multispectral imaging (MSI) acquires images in discrete spectral bands across the visible, near-infrared, and ultraviolet, typically 8-20 bands. Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) extends this to contiguous spectra (hundreds of bands) at each pixel, enabling full spectral reflectance curves for material identification.

Both techniques are non-invasive -- they require no physical sampling and can cover a full painting surface in a single acquisition session. The speed advantage over point spectroscopy (e.g., Raman, XRF) is significant for large works. Multispectral imaging has been applied to art conservation and art history since the early 1990s, migrating from aerospace remote-sensing platforms. [Liang 2011]

R(lambda) = L_r(lambda) / L_i(lambda)
// spectral reflectance: ratio of reflected to incident radiance at wavelength lambda
// each pigment has a characteristic reflectance curve used for identification

What It Reveals

  • Underdrawings and pentimenti (preparatory lines altered or abandoned)
  • Pigment identification via spectral library matching
  • Conservation state: degradation, retouching, previous restorations
  • Layer structure when combined with computational unmixing
  • Inscription legibility on faded documents and illuminated manuscripts

Pigment Identification

Spectral reflectance analysis is the central capability of MSI/HSI for material characterization. Each pigment absorbs and reflects at characteristic wavelengths -- lead white peaks in the near-infrared, azurite has a distinctive blue-region absorption feature, vermillion and red lead are separable spectrally despite visual similarity. [Liang 2011]

A significant limitation of purely spectroscopic methods is that organic pigments (lakes, indigo, organic yellows) have weak or featureless reflectance spectra and may require complementary Raman or GC/MS analysis for confirmation.

02 // X-Ray Fluorescence
MA-XRF Scanning

Physics

X-ray fluorescence works by irradiating the painting surface with a focused X-ray beam. Inner-shell electrons in target atoms are ejected (photoelectric effect); outer-shell electrons fill the vacancy and emit characteristic fluorescence X-rays whose energies are element-specific. Detection maps elemental distribution across the painting surface without physical sampling.

E_Ka = E_K - E_L // Ka fluorescence energy
// E_K = K-shell binding energy, E_L = L-shell energy
// each element has unique Ka, Kb, La... lines
// Hg -> Hg-La at 9.99 keV identifies vermillion (HgS)
// Pb -> Pb-La at 10.55 keV identifies lead white, red lead

Macro X-ray fluorescence (MA-XRF) scans the full painting surface with a motorized stage, generating per-pixel elemental maps at millimeter to sub-millimeter resolution. A deep learning variant (Science Advances 2024) operates at step sizes down to 50 micrometers. [Alfeld et al. 2024]

Key Limitation

XRF detects elements, not compounds or organic materials. Organic pigments (lakes, carbon black, organic yellows, indigo) are invisible to XRF because they contain only C, H, O, N -- elements too light to produce detectable fluorescence above background. Complementary techniques (Raman, FTIR, GC/MS) are required for a complete material account.

Authentication and Forgery Detection

MA-XRF scanning can reveal concealed paintings beneath the surface and resolve authenticity questions in works attributed to old masters. A 2017 Microchemical Journal study applied MA-XRF to two paintings under investigation -- one attributed to Francisco de Zurbarán, one to the Rubens workshop. [Saverwyns et al. 2017]

Zurbarán attribution
MA-XRF detected anatase TiO2 and Pigment Green 8 -- both post-1921 synthetics. A 17th-c. attribution is ruled out.
REJECTED
Rubens workshop attribution
Hidden canvas manufacturer's stamp detected beneath paint surface -- stamp dates the canvas to centuries after Rubens' death.
REJECTED

Neither stamp was visible in conventional X-radiography or infrared reflectography, establishing MA-XRF's unique contribution to physical evidence of anachronism.

Deep Learning XRF (2024)

Alfeld et al. (Science Advances 2024) trained a CNN on 500,000+ synthetic XRF spectra generated by Monte Carlo simulation (XMI-MSIM software), covering 57 pigments and compounds in a five-layer painting model. The network outputs estimates for 58 XRF spectral series. No real-world labeled training data was required. Validated on two Raphael panel paintings from the Baronci Altarpiece (1500-1501): "God the Father" (110 x 73 cm) and "Virgin Mary" (51 x 41 cm), scanned at step sizes down to 50 micrometers. Identified vermillion, azurite, copper green, and lapis lazuli. [Alfeld et al. 2024]

03 // Classical Imaging Methods
Infrared Reflectography

IRR exploits the transparency of carbon-based drawing media (charcoal, black chalk, ink) in the near-infrared (0.9-2.5 microns) while paint layers remain relatively opaque. Underdrawings invisible to the eye become legible in reflected NIR imaging.

Key diagnostic: the presence, absence, or style of underdrawing distinguishes workshop hands, reveals preparatory process, and can expose overpainted compositions (pentimenti). Artists who changed composition significantly mid-execution -- Raphael, Leonardo, Rubens -- show dense pentimenti detectable by IRR.

lambda_NIR = 900 - 2500 nm
// carbon black absorbs across NIR; lead white reflects
// InGaAs detector arrays optimized for 900-1700nm range
X-Ray Radiography

Conventional X-radiography records differential attenuation of penetrating X-rays by lead-bearing pigments (lead white, red lead, vermillion) relative to organic media and light pigments. Dense regions appear bright; thin or organic areas appear dark.

Reveals: paint layer thickness and distribution, canvas weave, ground preparation, repairs, relining, nail patterns (panel support dating), and compositional changes where lead-rich underpaint differs from the surface layer.

Notable limitation: X-radiography is insensitive to non-lead pigments and cannot resolve closely stacked layers. MA-XRF and neutron autoradiography address this by providing elemental selectivity.

UV Fluorescence

Ultraviolet illumination (310-380 nm) induces visible fluorescence in many organic varnish layers, pigments, and binding media. Old natural resin varnishes fluoresce greenish; modern synthetic restorer's varnishes appear dark or purple; retouched areas appear as dark patches against the fluorescing original.

UV fluorescence is rapid, equipment-light, and is typically the first examination step in conservation assessment. It cannot provide material identification on its own -- it is a detection tool, not an identification tool.

04 // Neutron Autoradiography
Neutron Activation Analysis

Physics

Neutron autoradiography (NAA) exposes a painting to a thermal neutron flux in a research reactor. Stable isotopes in paint pigments absorb neutrons and become radioactive; they then emit beta and gamma radiation that exposes film or imaging plates placed against the painting surface. Different radioactive decay half-lives allow sequential imaging -- plates recorded at different decay intervals reveal different elemental distributions.

Phosphorus (P-32, t1/2 = 14.3 days) decays quickly and reveals early images; manganese (Mn-56, t1/2 = 2.58 h) decays immediately; arsenic (As-76, t1/2 = 26.3 h) and antimony (Sb-122) are intermediate. This multi-exposure protocol provides layer-selective elemental maps without cutting the painting.

A = sigma * phi * N
// A = activity (disintegrations/sec)
// sigma = neutron capture cross-section (barns)
// phi = neutron flux (n/cm^2/s)
// N = number of target atoms in sample

Key Limitation

The technique requires a nuclear research reactor. The Berlin neutron autoradiography project ended in 2019 when the BER III reactor at Helmholtz-Zentrum Berlin was permanently decommissioned. Ongoing access depends on institutions with active research reactors willing to provide beam time.

Landmark Institution: Staatliche Museen zu Berlin

The Gemäldegalerie neutron autoradiography project ran from 1983 to 2019, a 36-year collaboration between the Gemäldegalerie, the Rathgen-Forschungslabor (both Staatliche Museen zu Berlin), and Helmholtz-Zentrum Berlin (formerly Hahn-Meitner-Institut). The project analyzed 54 paintings from the Gemäldegalerie collection, including works by Titian, Rembrandt, and Vermeer. [SMB 2019]

Active NAA programs continue at the neutron reactor facilities of MIT Lincoln Lab, NIST Center for Neutron Research, and the Institut Laue-Langevin (Grenoble). Access is competitive; turn-around times are measured in months.

05 // Dendrochronology
Tree-Ring Dating of Panel Paintings

Method

Panel paintings on oak and other hardwoods contain the annual growth rings of the source tree embedded in the support material. Each ring's width reflects that year's growing conditions; the ring-width series forms a fingerprint that can be matched against regional dendrochronological reference chronologies extending back 2,000+ years for European oak.

Dating proceeds by measuring ring widths on the panel edge, constructing a floating sequence, and statistically crossdating it (t-statistics, Gleichläufigkeit) against reference chronologies to anchor the sequence to a calendar year. The outermost preserved ring gives a terminus post quem: the earliest possible date for the painting (after allowing time for wood seasoning, typically 2-10+ years).

t = (r * sqrt(n-2)) / sqrt(1-r^2)
// Baillie-Pilcher t-statistic for crossdating
// r = Pearson correlation of ring-width series
// n = number of overlapping rings
// t > 3.5 conventionally significant; t > 5 strong match

Reproducibility and Transparency

Dendrochronology is a reproducible science when protocols are rigorously applied -- the same ring sequence always crossdates to the same calendar position. However, reports that provide only a final date without the supporting ring-width data, reference chronologies used, and statistical crossdating values cannot be independently verified. [Domínguez-Delmás 2025]

As Domínguez-Delmás (2025) writes: "accepting the result becomes an act of faith that reduces the science to an opaque black box." Full data transparency is a prerequisite for valid authentication use.

Case Study: GNM Rembrandt Panel

A 2025 replication study by Domínguez-Delmás (Nature / Humanities and Social Sciences Communications) re-examined the oak panel support of a painting attributed to Rembrandt in the Germanisches Nationalmuseum, Nuremberg (GNM). [Domínguez-Delmás 2025]

Total rings (GNM panel) 210
Outermost ring date 1606 CE
Estimated production window 1617 -- 1635
Match to Rijksmuseum panel Same source tree
Rijksmuseum accession SK-A-3934

The replication also identified two errors in Klein's original 1990s measurements of the same panel: one ring was missing from the series and one ring was counted twice in the early portion of the sequence. Despite these measurement errors, the overall dating conclusion was confirmed -- demonstrating both the robustness of the crossdating statistical framework and the importance of independent replication.

06 // Gas Chromatography / Mass Spectrometry
Paint Medium Analysis

Chemistry and Protocol

Paint binding media (drying oils, egg tempera, proteinaceous glues, resin varnishes) are organic molecules degraded by age, light, and heat into characteristic breakdown products. GC/MS hydrolysis-based analysis converts these polymeric binders into monomers -- fatty acids from oils, amino acids from proteins -- which are then separated by gas chromatography and identified by mass spectrometry.

The technique requires micro-sampling: typically 50-500 micrograms extracted with a needle or scalpel from areas of existing damage or loss. It is therefore not non-invasive, but samples at the microgram level are effectively invisible and ethically accepted for authentication.

P/S ratio = palmitic / stearic acid peak area
// P/S ~1.5 = walnut or poppy oil
// P/S ~2.0 = linseed oil (typical ground)
// elevated dicarboxylic acids = late-stage oxidative crosslinking

Amino Acid Derivatization: Solvent Optimization

Amino acid analysis by GC/MS requires derivatization (typically silylation with MTBSTFA) to increase analyte volatility. Fico et al. (2018) demonstrated that replacing pyridine with anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF) as the derivatization solvent significantly improves calibration linearity for proline and hydroxyproline -- the two diagnostic amino acids for collagen-based and egg-based paint media. [Fico et al. 2018]

Proline r² (pyridine)
0.89
Proline r² (DMF) -- improved
0.99
Hydroxyproline r² (pyridine)
0.72
Hydroxyproline r² (DMF) -- improved
0.95

Pyridine can retard MTBSTFA silylation and generate side-reaction artifacts for hydrophilic amino acids. The DMF improvement is particularly significant for distinguishing collagen (animal glue sizing) from egg albumen or yolk -- both identifiable by their characteristic hydroxyproline content.

07a // Raman Spectroscopy
Molecular Fingerprinting

Raman spectroscopy irradiates the painting surface with a monochromatic laser; inelastic scattering of photons produces frequency shifts (Stokes/anti-Stokes bands) characteristic of specific molecular vibrational modes. Each pigment has a Raman fingerprint that is unambiguous at the molecular level.

Key advantage over XRF: Raman identifies compounds and molecular structure, not just elements. It can distinguish hematite from red ochre, malachite from verdigris, and -- critically -- detect organic pigments (ultramarine, indigo, organic lake pigments) that are XRF-invisible.

Micro-Raman allows point analysis at 1-5 micron spatial resolution. Portable Raman systems (e.g., Renishaw, B&W Tek) enable in-situ museum analysis without transport. Key limitation: fluorescence interference from aged varnish can swamp the Raman signal; NIR lasers (785 nm, 1064 nm) mitigate this.

07b // Digital Microscopy and FORS
Surface and Optical Methods

Digital microscopy (10-400x) reveals paint surface texture, impasto, craquelure pattern (age crack networks), abrasion, and retouching at a scale not visible to the naked eye. Craquelure age and pattern can distinguish 17th-century paint films (characteristic island cracking) from 20th-century imitations.

Fiber optic reflectance spectroscopy (FORS) is a portable point spectroscopy method covering 350-2500 nm. It produces the same reflectance curve used in MSI/HSI but at a single spatial point. Used for rapid in-gallery triage, varnish characterization, and spot verification before committing to full imaging.

OCT (optical coherence tomography) is an emerging method providing cross-sectional depth profiling of paint layers at micron resolution without sampling -- analogous to medical ultrasound but at optical wavelengths. Currently limited to transparent or semi-transparent layers (varnish, glazes).

08 // AI and Machine Learning Methods
Computational Pigment Analysis

1D-CNN vs. SVM (Hyperspectral)

For per-pixel pigment classification from hyperspectral data, 1D convolutional neural networks operating on the spectral dimension outperform conventional spectral classifiers. Kleynhans et al. (2023) demonstrated on the 14th-century illuminated manuscript Laudario di Sant'Agnese (c. 1340): [PMC10006919]

1D-CNN vs. SAM margin
+2.1%
1D-CNN vs. SVM margin
+6.4%

Test set: 25 paint classes, 16,683 spectra across 4 paintings. VNIR hyperspectral data. 10-fold cross-validation.

Neural networks applied to hyperspectral data also improve visibility of pentimenti and underdrawings. Striova et al. (2018) applied an ANN to multispectral data of Manet and Titian paintings and demonstrated improved pentimenti visibility. [PMC10006919]

Spectral Unmixing (Quantitative)

Beyond presence/absence classification, hyperspectral neural networks can perform Paint Component Unmixing: quantitatively decomposing a pixel spectrum into its constituent pigment components (a regression task, not a classification task). Output is a per-pixel abundance map for each pigment -- analogous to a concentration map. [PMC10006919]

pixel_spectrum = SUM( a_i * R_i(lambda) )
// a_i = abundance (mixing coefficient) for pigment i
// R_i(lambda) = pure pigment reflectance spectrum
// linear mixing model: valid for optically thin layers
// non-linear extensions required for opaque layer stacks

Fukumoto et al.'s encoder-decoder architecture with a KM-model decoder estimates both pigment thickness and mixing ratio per pixel from spectral data. Rohani et al.'s two-branch network yields semi-quantitative abundance mapping.

Caution: real-painting accuracy is lower than synthetic-data benchmarks. Scarcity of labeled real-painting training data remains the field's primary bottleneck.

CNN on Synthetic XRF (MA-XRF)

Jones et al. (npj Heritage Science 2022) trained CNNs on artificially generated XRF spectra using the Fundamental Parameter (FP) method, covering 15 pigment classes with a synthetic dataset of 3,000 spectra. Initial accuracy on synthetic data was 100%, but dropped to ~55% on real historical spectra due to spectral variation. Transfer learning (retraining the final layer with real spectra) recovered performance to ~96%. [Jones et al. 2022]

The Alfeld et al. (2024) approach scales this dramatically: 500,000+ Monte Carlo synthetic spectra, 57 pigments, five-layer painting model -- no real-world labeled data required. [Alfeld et al. 2024]

09 // Computational Attribution
Deep Learning for Artist Attribution

ResNet50 + SVM Pipeline (Raphael Study)

Ugail et al. (npj Heritage Science 2023) developed a hybrid attribution architecture: ResNet50 pretrained on ImageNet with the top fully connected layer removed, feeding extracted deep features into a linear-kernel SVM binary classifier (Raphael vs. non-Raphael). This hybrid design -- deep learning for feature extraction, conventional ML for classification -- is intentional: the SVM provides a more interpretable decision boundary than an end-to-end neural classifier. [Ugail et al. 2023]

f(x) = SIGN( w^T * phi(x) + b )
// x = painting image
// phi(x) = ResNet50 feature vector (last conv layer)
// w, b = SVM hyperplane learned on training set
// linear kernel: no additional hyperparameter gamma

The model was applied to "Madonna della rosa" to assess sole vs. workshop authorship. The paper interprets the result as evidence of possible workshop/assistant collaboration rather than sole Raphael authorship -- consistent with art-historical accounts of Raphael's large workshop production ca. 1518.

Scope, Limitations, and Verification Status

The specific accuracy figures for this pipeline (98% binary classification) and the exact probability output for "Madonna della rosa" (0.57) could not be independently verified through the adversarial review process and are therefore excluded from this reference. The architectural description -- ResNet50 feature extraction plus linear-kernel SVM -- is confirmed.

Broader limitations of computational attribution:

  • Training set size is severely constrained for old-master authentication
  • Authenticated training data may itself contain misattributions
  • Stylistic features learned by CNNs are not directly interpretable as artistic decisions
  • Photographic reproduction quality, varnish state, and color calibration all introduce noise
  • Binary classification (Raphael / not-Raphael) cannot model workshop continua

State of the Field (2025)

npj Heritage Science published a state-of-the-art review of deep learning for MA-XRF in 2025 (DOI 10.1038/s40494-025-01924-3), covering both spectral unmixing and pigment classification. The consensus: synthetic training data generation (Monte Carlo, FP method) combined with transfer learning is the most viable approach given the scarcity of labeled real-painting data. Physics-informed architectures are promising but have not yet demonstrated superiority over purely data-driven methods on benchmark real-painting datasets. [npj Heritage Science 2025]
